Manuscript: Personalized Real-Time Blood Glucose Prediction via Hedging-Based Machine Learning
Recommendation: Reject in its current form; reconsider only after correction of the method and a reproducible reanalysis.
The objective is worthwhile: individualized CGM forecasting without population pretraining could be useful. However, the revision does not yet establish its principal scientific claim—that the proposed online allocation mechanism reliably tracks changing expert performance and improves clinically relevant prediction. Conflicting results, unexplained analysis populations, a defective sharing recursion, and an invalid theorem prevent a reliable assessment of the reported benefit. These are problems of scientific validity, not presentation.
The revision makes several substantive improvements: participant-level paired analyses and simple baselines are added; the forecast is now explicitly a weighted mean; delayed outcome feedback is represented; and the NODE derivative is now unconstrained. The headline 5-hour percentage reductions agree with Tables 3 and 5. These corrections are welcome, but the new material introduces or reveals further inconsistencies.
Scope: This report evaluates the supplied 38-page PDF, checks selected primary sources, and independently audits the stated mathematics and table arithmetic. It does not reproduce the patient-level experiments or inspect the study implementation. Page references below use the manuscript’s printed numbering; PDF viewer page numbers are one greater. No supplementary file was provided.

2. The effective sample sizes undermine the headline population claims
Evidence: abstract, Table 1, captions to Tables 3–5, and p. 31. The manuscript repeatedly presents an evaluation of 100 Weinstock and 45 CGMacros participants. Table 1 instead uses 63 Weinstock participants at 30 minutes, 55–61 at 2 hours, and 46–47 at 5 hours; CGMacros uses 44 throughout. Thus, the headline 77% improvement at 5 hours is 36/47, and the 73% figure is 32/44, not improvement percentages demonstrated across all 100 and 45 participants.
This is consequential because 53 of the nominal 100 Weinstock participants do not enter the full-history 5-hour paired analysis. If they also do not enter the main performance table, that table is mislabeled; if they do, the descriptive and inferential results concern different populations. Loss of participants may depend on missingness, unstable glucose, model failure, or available recording length, each of which can change the apparent benefit.
Required analysis: provide a participant and segment flow diagram, exclusion reasons by horizon and model, and counts of eligible/scored forecasts and hypoglycemic episodes. Compare included and excluded participants. Evaluate all methods on identical participant–origin pairs and report a common-participant sensitivity analysis across horizons. Missing or failed model outputs need an explicit policy; silently dropping difficult cases is not an acceptable evaluation strategy.
3. The dataset provenance contains substantive factual discrepancies
Evidence: pp. 26–27 and Table 14. The manuscript states that the Weinstock recordings extend up to six months with a median of 180 days. The original study describes 14 days of blinded CGM, using two 7-day SEVEN PLUS sensors, and enrolled 201 participants. This discrepancy changes the available training data, the meaning of full history, and the credibility of claims about sustained adaptation.

The claimed GlucoBench-standardized subset of 100 participants, comprising 50 cases and 50 controls, also needs documentation. GlucoBench Table 2 lists 200 raw and 192 processed Weinstock subjects, rather than the stated 100-person subset. A custom subset may be legitimate, but its identifiers, selection rule, and demographic summaries must be supplied. The device descriptions also need reconciliation against the actual source files; the original clinical paper and GlucoBench label the hardware differently.

4. The preprocessing does not establish a causal real-time evaluation
Evidence: p. 27. Explicit delayed-loss bookkeeping is an improvement, but it does not resolve preprocessing leakage. Piecewise linear interpolation ordinarily uses observations on both sides of a gap. For example, a Libre value constructed at 10:05 from readings at 10:00 and 10:15 would be unavailable to a system forecasting at 10:05. The same issue applies to short missing-data gaps. A chronological train/test loop cannot repair future information already introduced into its inputs.
The manuscript does not establish that this occurred in the code; it fails to specify an availability-respecting preprocessing procedure. Required analysis: audit measurement time and availability time separately, use only information available at each forecast origin, and rerun the evaluation with causal gap handling. A primary analysis on native, actually observed Dexcom timestamps would avoid the need to invent 5-minute Libre observations. Mark imputed targets and report performance on observed targets separately, since interpolated targets can artificially smooth the prediction problem.
Bounding predictions can be a sensible constraint, but capping the reference readings to [40, 400] mg/dL changes the outcome being predicted. State how many observations were capped, distinguish sensor censoring/artifact removal from valid extreme readings, and show sensitivity to the treatment of extremes. The loss bound in a theorem is not, by itself, a scientific justification for modifying the clinical reference data.
5. Algorithm 1 does not implement the claimed Fixed-Share mechanism
Evidence: Eqs. 9–14 and Algorithm 1, pp. 25–26. The algorithm accumulates each expert’s entire historical loss and recomputes its exponential weight from that total. It then mixes the normalized weights with a uniform distribution. Crucially, the shared probability mass is not used in the next persistent weight update. This is a cumulative-loss exponential forecaster with output mixing, rather than the recursive Fixed-Share procedure invoked by the scientific argument.
Consequently, an expert that performed poorly early in a record must overcome its historical loss disadvantage before it receives substantial weight. Decaying only the scale denominator does not forget those old losses. Indeed, reducing that denominator can sharpen commitment to the historically favored expert. The mechanism therefore does not establish the promised recovery after physiological regime changes.
A controlled calculation illustrates the issue. With five bounded constant experts and one change in the correct expert, Algorithm 1 incurs normalized deterministic regret of approximately 999.34 over 2,000 rounds, while the manuscript’s claimed expression is 18.20. Changing the sharing schedule to the theorem’s 1/t still gives approximately 998.61. The construction and scope of this diagnostic are given in Appendix A. Required correction: reconcile the implementation and pseudocode, implement the intended recursion if necessary, and rerun the experiments and adaptation analyses.
Source: Adamskiy et al., A Closer Look at Adaptive Regret: Fixed-Share formulation
6. The revised regret theorem is still false as stated
Evidence: Theorem 1 and Eqs. 4–8, pp. 24–25. Bounded loss fixes the units of the earlier expression but does not make the claimed logarithmic bound valid for the expected loss of randomized expert selection. With two endpoint-valued experts and independent fair endpoint-valued outcomes, the expected regret against the best fixed expert is approximately 12.61 times the maximum loss at T = 1,000, whereas Eq. 5 allows only 7.60 times that loss. This directly contradicts the stated theorem even with no expert switches; Appendix B gives the derivation.
This objection is specific to the stated randomized-loss guarantee. Appropriately designed deterministic aggregation under bounded square loss can obtain logarithmic guarantees using mixability or exp-concavity. The paper must establish such a result for its actual forecast and update rule. Jensen’s inequality cannot transfer a guarantee whose premise is false, nor does it establish that averaging beats the best individual expert.
Further gaps remain: the theorem uses sharing 1/t while the implementation uses 1/t²; the adaptive decaying scale and horizon-dependent delayed feedback are not covered by a proof; and the best expert at each round may switch on the order of T times. The assumed parameter convergence rates are not established for repeatedly trained neural models, dependent nonstationary CGM data, or fixed-length rolling windows. The decomposition does not justify unconditional convergence of total average regret to zero. Required correction: state and prove a valid result for the implemented algorithm, or remove unsupported guarantees and present the method as an empirical proposal.
7. The experiments do not isolate the scientific value of time-varying allocation
Evidence: Table 2, Figure 5, and the central adaptation claim. Adding persistence and uniform averaging is useful, but improvement over a weak or incoherently evaluated comparator does not demonstrate that expert switching is the mechanism of benefit. HBML could mainly identify a strong participant-specific model or combine correlated forecasts; neither explanation requires tracking physiological regimes.
The decisive controls are a prospectively selected single expert, fixed participant-specific convex weights fitted on development data, ordinary recursive exponential weighting without sharing, correctly implemented Fixed-Share, and a simple recent-loss selection or weighting rule. Ablate sharing and scale decay separately while keeping exactly the same expert predictions. Compare with persistence and a simple local trend/autoregressive forecast on the same forecast origins.
Report how quickly weights and losses respond to predefined changes, how often HBML meaningfully departs from NODE, and whether that departure improves subsequent prediction. The per-participant static best expert in Table 2 should be identified as an oracle if selected using the entire evaluation period; it is informative diagnostically but is not a deployable baseline. Cumulative regret and local excess-loss curves are needed if empirical no-regret behavior remains a stated result.
8. Model selection and evaluation are not sufficiently separated
Evidence: p. 26 and the expert-model methods. The manuscript says the 1/t² sharing schedule was chosen for superior empirical stability and that a default decay of 0.20 performed robustly. It does not show the sensitivity results or identify data reserved for those choices. Without a clear separation, the same participants and periods may have selected the method and estimated its advantage. Two independently sourced cohorts do not automatically constitute independent validation of a locked pipeline.
Specify the development/validation/evaluation split for all choices: expert architecture, lag features, training windows, refitting frequency, preprocessing thresholds, loss normalization, sharing and decay. Fit supervised training examples only when their future labels would already be available. Clarify whether “context” means input length, training-set length, or both, and how the 500-point burn-in interacts with rolling windows and sequence boundaries.
Required analysis: lock the pipeline before evaluating held-out participants or later chronological blocks, or use a nested design appropriate to the data volume. Give constituent experts a defensible development/tuning budget and report repeated-seed variability for stochastic training. A heterogeneous ensemble can appear strong simply because some constituent implementations are poorly configured; the present protocol does not adequately rule that out.
9. The paired analysis needs a coherent estimand and multiplicity strategy
Evidence: Table 1 and p. 31. Participant-level paired comparisons are the correct starting point and avoid treating thousands of overlapping forecasts as independent participants. However, the text counts 20 significant configurations, while the displayed p-values give 21 of 24 at an unadjusted 0.05 threshold. No multiplicity strategy is stated for configurations, subgroup analyses, or comparisons with all models. The central endpoint and testing family should be specified, with adjusted results or explicitly exploratory interpretation.
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test does not directly test the mean paired RMSE reduction reported in the headline; location interpretations require assumptions about the paired differences. Checking normality of marginal participant errors is not the relevant check for a paired t-test. Specify what each test and interval estimates. A positive bootstrap mean interval alongside a nonsignificant Wilcoxon test is not automatically a contradiction, because they address different features of the distribution.
The “best expert” in Table 1 is selected by the lowest cohort mean on the evaluation data. Treating this selected comparator as fixed leaves the selection step outside the reported uncertainty. Prespecify it from development data or incorporate selection into a justified resampling/validation procedure. Report participant-level differences and intervals for the proportion benefiting; preserve participants as the independent units, and use dependence-aware within-person resampling if additional temporal uncertainty is estimated.
Statistical significance also needs a practical interpretation. At full history, the CGMacros 30-minute mean advantage is 0.22 mg/dL and the median advantage only 0.02 mg/dL. Weinstock’s corresponding 30-minute and 2-hour differences are 0.08 and 0.17 mg/dL, with Wilcoxon p = 0.1185 and 0.5786. “Matched” performance is not a demonstrated equivalence or noninferiority result without a margin and an appropriate analysis.
10. The regime analysis does not demonstrate physiological adaptation
Evidence: Figure 4, p. 6 and p. 30. Splitting ties fractionally is a useful correction, and a convex mean can now genuinely outperform all component forecasts at an individual time point. Nevertheless, Jensen’s inequality guarantees performance no worse than the weighted average of component losses, not the minimum component loss. The explanation invoking Jensen to establish strict superiority over every expert is mathematically incorrect.
A frequency-of-winning plot discards the magnitude of errors: frequent negligible wins can coexist with rare severe failures. It also does not show that changing weights causes the benefit. Report paired error differences, participant and event counts, and uncertainty within each regime, with direct comparison to fixed weights. Define whether labels use information at the forecast origin or target; retrospective target-based stratification can be descriptive, but cannot establish prospective regime recognition.
The hierarchical labels also need complete operational rules. For example, sustained flat hyperglycemia may satisfy none of the preceding categories yet fall into the default “stable euglycemia” category. Specify how persistent out-of-range glucose is handled, and test sensitivity to the rate-of-change and variability thresholds. These are constructed glucose-pattern labels, not independently verified physiological mechanisms.
11. Clinical usefulness is not established by average RMSE and Zone A+B percentages
Evidence: pp. 5 and 21, Tables 6 and 10–12. The revised discussion correctly distinguishes Clarke error-grid classification from event detection. However, the manuscript still gives clinical interpretations that exceed the evidence. Zone A+B proportions pooled over ordinary glucose readings can obscure performance during rare hypoglycemic events and do not measure advance warning, alarm burden, or the benefit of acting on a forecast.
For an early-warning claim, define the event, prediction window and alarm rule, and report event sensitivity, positive predictive value, false alarms per person-day, lead time, and missed prolonged/severe events, with participant-level uncertainty. Compare these outcomes with the strongest practical baselines. Report errors by observed glucose range and rate of change, including the number of participants responsible for dangerous errors. These analyses are more informative than another global significance test.
The statement that all 0.93% of Weinstock Zone E failures occurred during postprandial spikes or rescue-carbohydrate treatment is not supported by the stated univariate data and absence of synchronized meal/insulin records. Glucose patterns alone cannot verify those causes. Supply an adjudication protocol and independent annotations, or label the attribution as a hypothesis. Likewise, four endpoint forecasts do not establish a continuous trajectory envelope or its uncertainty. Treatment-planning claims need calibrated uncertainty and action-specific validation. The demonstrated target is future CGM sensor glucose, not independently validated future blood glucose.
12. A reproducible experiment specification is necessary before further interpretation
Evidence: Table 14, Algorithm 1, and p. 31. Table 14 describes contexts of 1 hour, 2 hours and full history with four horizons, including 1 hour. The results instead use 6-, 12-, 24-hour and full-history contexts with three horizons. The statistics section repeats “2 datasets × 4 horizons × 3 history contexts.” Although both products equal 24, they describe different experiments. This ambiguity must be resolved before any claim is reproducible.
Release the exact code commit, environment, configurations, seeds, participant/segment manifests, cached issued predictions and weights, and scripts generating all tables and figures. Specify forecast stride, endpoint versus trajectory scoring, forecast caching across refits, horizon-specific weight updates, clipping, failure handling, and resets after gaps. Explain whether the changed NODE output activation and aggregation description correct documentation or represent new experiments, and map each result to the implementation that generated it. Supplementary Table S1 is referenced but was not included in the supplied PDF.
The runtime additions are useful but do not establish smartphone or embedded feasibility. A mean ± SD of 5.1551 ± 0.2306 seconds for a step with all models refitted is not a worst-case latency bound. Report hardware, record length, memory, and tail latency, and test the stated deployment device before claiming compatibility. Operational feasibility does not resolve the scientific validity issues above.
Minimum evidence needed for reconsideration
The paper needs a corrected and fully specified online algorithm, defensible theoretical claims, an auditable cohort and preprocessing pipeline, and one coherent reanalysis that reproduces every reported number. The central empirical test should establish incremental benefit over strong fixed and adaptive baselines on data not used to choose the method. Clinically focused error and event analyses should then determine whether any remaining gain is useful. Until these steps are complete, the current evidence is insufficient for the manuscript’s principal claims.
Appendix A. Diagnostic of the stated update rule
This is an independently constructed mathematical stress test of Algorithm 1, not a reanalysis of the CGM datasets and not a claim about the behavior of unretrieved study code. It uses immediate feedback to isolate the weight update; adding clinical forecast delay is not a remedy for the missing recursive sharing.
Take five constant forecasts: one expert predicts 40 mg/dL, and four predict 400 mg/dL. Let the outcome be 40 for the first 1,000 rounds and 400 for the next 1,000. All predictions and outcomes satisfy the paper’s bounds. Divide all losses by 129,600, so the expert losses are 0 or 1. At every round the maximum expert loss is 1; therefore the decaying maximum is also 1, including at the default decay of 0.20.
Initialize uniform probabilities and zero cumulative losses. At round t, issue the weighted mean, observe the outcome, add each expert’s loss to its cumulative total, set weights proportional to the exponential of minus that total, and form the next probabilities by mixing with the uniform distribution using the stated next-round sharing rate. This follows Algorithm 1’s weight update. A comparator that chooses the 40-valued expert in the first block and a 400-valued expert in the second has zero loss and one switch.

Appendix B. Counterexample to the randomized-loss theorem
Use two experts that always predict the two endpoints, 40 and 400 mg/dL. Independently at each round, choose the outcome to be either endpoint with probability one half. Normalize square loss by the maximum loss. Conditional on any past history, every randomized choice of one of these experts has expected loss one half. Thus the expected cumulative randomized loss is T/2.

If the claimed bound held for every outcome sequence, it would also hold after averaging over these sequences. It does not. This refutes the expected randomized-loss theorem as stated. It does not rule out logarithmic guarantees for a properly designed deterministic square-loss aggregation rule, whose loss is different from the randomized expert loss in Eq. 4.
